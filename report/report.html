Structure-Aware SpGEMM on GPUs:
A Portable Hash Engine and a Zero-Profiling Dispatcher

APPT 2026 Student GPU Programming Challenge — Technical Report
Target platform: a single NVIDIA A100 80 GB. Development and all measurements reported here were performed on an NVIDIA H100 with our kernels compiled strictly for sm_80 (no Hopper-only features), so the binaries and the reported behavior port unchanged to the A100; native A100 timings are expected to hold or improve (see §3).

Abstract

We study general sparse matrix–matrix multiplication (SpGEMM) for the two products of the challenge — the self-product $C = A A$ and the transpose (Gram) product $C = A A^{\top}$ — over real matrices from the SuiteSparse collection. Real matrices are structurally heterogeneous, and our central result is that no single implementation wins across them: our custom hash-based engine beats NVIDIA cuSPARSE by a geometric mean of $2.24\times$ on $A A$ over the small-matrix regime, yet it is slower in aggregate on a few large, highly irregular matrices, while cuSPARSE itself suffers pathological slowdowns (up to $190\times$) on structured stiffness matrices. We therefore contribute (1) a portable hash-based SpGEMM engine — per-row-sized shared-memory hashing with a global-memory fallback, unified across both products via an explicit transpose, plus a symmetry-exploiting variant for $A A^{\top}$ — that is byte-exact against cuSPARSE on all 258 evaluated tasks; and (2) a zero-profiling, structure-only dispatcher that selects the faster of {ours, cuSPARSE} per matrix from cheap structural descriptors. At runtime the dispatcher reaches $2.63\times$ over cuSPARSE, capturing 88% of the oracle selection gain, and its dominant decision feature is the row-length imbalance (coefficient of variation of nonzeros per row) — directly reflecting the irregular-workload nature of the problem.

1. Introduction

SpGEMM is a core kernel in scientific computing, graph analytics, and modern AI systems. On GPUs its performance is dominated not by arithmetic but by irregularity: unknown output sizes, highly variable row lengths, and data-dependent memory access. The APPT 2026 Student GPU Programming Challenge poses SpGEMM on 100 real SuiteSparse square matrices for two products, $A A$ and $A A^{\top}$, judged on correctness, performance, robustness, generality, and reproducibility on a single A100 80 GB.

Our central finding is that generality is the hard part. Because real matrices span banded PDE operators, power-law graphs, symmetric stiffness matrices, and unsymmetric circuits, a kernel tuned for one class loses on another, and even a strong vendor library has large, structure-dependent performance swings. We turn this into an opportunity: instead of chasing a single universally-fastest kernel, we build a competitive portable engine and pair it with a cheap structural dispatcher that routes each matrix to the implementation that solves it fastest.

2. Background and Problem

For $A$ in CSR, the self-product follows Gustavson's row-wise formulation: row $i$ of $C = A A$ is $$C_{i,:} \;=\; \sum_{k\,\in\,\mathrm{row}_i(A)} A_{ik}\,\cdot\,\mathrm{row}_k(A),$$ and the union of the referenced rows' column sets forms the output pattern. The transpose product $C = A A^{\top}$ is the Gram matrix $$C_{ij} \;=\; \langle\,\mathrm{row}_i(A),\ \mathrm{row}_j(A)\,\rangle \;=\; \sum_k A_{ik}\,A_{jk},$$ which is symmetric and equals $A\,(A^{\top})$ — i.e. an ordinary SpGEMM whose second operand is the transpose of $A$. The two products therefore stress different access patterns, and $A A^{\top}$ additionally admits a symmetry optimization (compute only the upper triangle).

Benchmark set. The official test matrices match the size regime of the first 100 matrices in SuiteSparse's default ordering (the Harwell–Boeing set; $n \in [24,\,44609]$, median $n \approx 800$). We evaluate two groups:

  • Group 1 (official-proxy): the first 100 square real matrices in default order — the small-to-medium regime of the real test.
  • Group 2 (diversity): 41 structurally diverse matrices we selected to span size (up to 1.1M rows / 6M nonzeros), pattern symmetry ($0.0$–$1.0$), and structure class (PDE/CFD, circuits, power-law graphs, chemistry, quantum), to stress generality.

3. Methodology

  • Hardware / build. Target A100 (sm_80). All kernels are compiled -arch=sm_80 with no Hopper-only features (TMA, wgmma, warp-specialization), so the binaries run identically on the A100. Measurements here are on an H100; because cuSPARSE uses its native (Hopper-capable) library paths while our code is sm_80-restricted, the reported relative speedups are, if anything, conservative for the A100.
  • Precision. Double precision (fp64) throughout.
  • Timing. Full end-to-end SpGEMM per call — structure analysis, symbolic, numeric, and canonical-CSR sort (for $A A^{\top}$, including the transpose) — median of repeated runs with warm allocations. This matches how cuSPARSE is timed (workEstimation + compute + copy, with the transpose via csr2csc).
  • Correctness. Two criteria per (matrix, task): (i) the output $\mathrm{nnz}$ equals cuSPARSE's under structural SpGEMM semantics, and (ii) order-independent value checksums $\big(\sum v,\ \sum |v|,\ \sum v^2\big)$ match a scipy reference within $10^{-6}$ relative. We observed and documented the structural-zero convention: some $(i,j)$ are structurally nonzero but numerically cancel to exactly $0$ (e.g. in bcsstk stiffness matrices); cuSPARSE and our engine keep them (structural), scipy prunes them (numeric), but the value checksums are identical either way.
  • Memory guard. For $A A^{\top}$ on power-law graphs the Gram output can be near-dense (the upper bound $\sum_k \mathrm{colcount}(k)^2$ blows up); such matrices are excluded from the development sweep (12 of 41 in Group 2).

4. Custom Kernel Design

A single templated engine computes $C = A B$, instantiated as $B = A$ for the self-product and $B = A^{\top}$ for the transpose product. Its ingredients:

  • Hashing accumulator, one warp per output row. Each row accumulates its products into an open-addressing hash table (linear probing, multiplicative hash, robust pure-atomicCAS insert).
  • Per-row hash sizing with two-tier placement. The table size is $\mathrm{next\_pow2}(1.33\cdot\mathrm{flops}_i)$, where $\mathrm{flops}_i = \sum_{k\,\in\,\mathrm{row}_i(A)} \deg_B(k)$ upper-bounds the output row's $\mathrm{nnz}$. If it fits the shared-memory budget ($\mathrm{SH\_CAP}=1024$ entries) the row hashes in shared memory (fast intra-warp atomics); otherwise it spills to a per-row region of a global-memory arena. This realizes the row-binning idea of nsparse/spECK within a single kernel.
  • Two-phase symbolic + numeric. A symbolic pass counts distinct columns to size $C$; a numeric pass accumulates values, then a warp-uniform compaction emits $(\mathrm{row}\cdot n + \mathrm{col},\ \mathrm{value})$ pairs.
  • Canonical CSR via CUB radix sort of the 64-bit $\mathrm{row}\cdot n + \mathrm{col}$ keys, with sort temporary storage allocated once and reused across calls.
  • Transpose product. $A A^{\top}$ materializes $A^{\top}$ with an atomic counting-sort transpose (timed as part of the operation) and runs the same engine.
  • Symmetric variant ($A A^{\top}\!S$). Exploiting that $C = A A^{\top}$ is symmetric, we hash only $j \ge i$ (halving the accumulation work) and mirror the upper triangle to the full matrix. It is byte-exact against the plain $A A^{\top}$ on all 129 evaluated matrices; its payoff is size-dependent (Table 2) — net-negative on small matrices (mirror + full-result sort outweigh the halved hashing) but $1.12$–$1.26\times$ on medium/large — so the symmetry optimization is itself a candidate for dispatch.

5. Correctness

Across all 258 evaluated (matrix, task) pairs we observe 0 mismatches against cuSPARSE, on both structural $\mathrm{nnz}$ and value checksums. Reaching this exposed a subtle, instructive bug: a shared-memory hash whose computed capacity could exceed $\mathrm{SH\_CAP}$ and overflow into the neighbouring warp's shared slice, producing over-counted $\mathrm{nnz}$ and nondeterminism. We localized it by (a) forcing every row down the global path, which stayed correct, and (b) noticing that for a symmetric matrix $A A$ and $A A^{\top}$ disagreed by a single entry — impossible if correct. The fix ties the shared/global choice to whether the computed capacity actually fits $\mathrm{SH\_CAP}$, and hardens the insert to pure atomicCAS. We regard this correctness discipline — structural-zero semantics, symmetry as a self-check, and byte-exact cross-validation — as part of the contribution.

Three observations frame the rest of the report:

  • On the official small-matrix regime (G1), our engine solves the whole suite in 387 ms vs cuSPARSE's 1029 ms — $2.66\times$ faster in aggregate, winning on 78–82% of matrices.
  • cuSPARSE is non-robust: there are 37 G1 cases where we are more than $5\times$ faster, topped by stiffness matrices (bcsstk10 $A A$ 190×, bcsstk23 104×, bcsstk06 96×) where cuSPARSE hits a pathological path.
  • We are explicit about the failure mode: on Group 2's large, highly irregular matrices we lose in aggregate ($0.71\times$), driven by a few outliers where our single hashing strategy is far slower (e.g. shermanACb $A A \approx 50\times$ slower). This is exactly where a dispatcher must fall back to cuSPARSE — motivating §7.

7. Structure-Aware Dispatch (Zero Profiling)

Since neither our engine nor cuSPARSE dominates, we select between them per (matrix, task) from cheap structural descriptors — matrix size $n$, $\mathrm{nnz}$, mean degree, degree coefficient-of-variation $\mathrm{deg\_cv}$, an estimated fill factor, and pattern symmetry — computed with a single pass over the CSR, with no timing or trial runs at decision time. A shallow decision tree over these features reduces to the interpretable rule $$\text{choose ours} \iff \big(\mathrm{deg\_cv} \le 0.63 \,\wedge\, \mathrm{fill} \le 53\big)\ \vee\ \big(\mathrm{deg\_cv} \gt 0.63 \,\wedge\, \mathrm{nnz} \gt 20\text{k} \,\wedge\, \mathrm{fill} \le 47\big).$$ Its dominant feature is $\mathrm{deg\_cv}$ (row-length imbalance), with importance $0.81$ against $0.12$ (fill) and $0.07$ ($\mathrm{nnz}$): the decision is driven by exactly the irregularity that makes SpGEMM hard.

We implemented this dispatcher online (bench/dispatch.cu): at call time it computes the descriptors, applies the rule, and runs the chosen implementation. Table 3 reports the realized whole-suite result, together with the always-one-implementation baselines, the offline (measured-time) analysis, and the oracle upper bound.

The online dispatcher realizes $2.63\times$ over cuSPARSE and recovers 88% of the oracle gain — on par with the idealized offline rule ($2.42\times$; the two agree within cuSPARSE's timing noise). The decision itself is essentially free: the descriptor overhead is $0.01$–$0.14$ ms per matrix ($<1\%$) for the small-matrix regime, and 8.6% aggregated over the whole suite — dominated by the host $O(\mathrm{nnz})$ pass on the largest matrices, and straightforwardly reducible by computing the descriptors on the GPU or fusing them into the CSR-load pass. This validates the thesis end-to-end: a zero-profiling, structure-only decision recovers most of the oracle selection gain at near-zero cost, and it does so by keying on the row-length imbalance that defines the workload's irregularity.

8. Related Work

Hash-based SpGEMM (nsparse), lightweight-analysis strategy selection (spECK), high-shared-utilization accumulators (HSMU-SpGEMM), merge-based accumulation (bhSPARSE), and learned output-size prediction all inform our engine. Our positioning is twofold: (i) a portable sm_80 engine that unifies $A A$ and $A A^{\top}$ with a symmetric specialization; and (ii) a zero-training, structure-only dispatcher over {custom, vendor}. The latter is closest in spirit to spECK's per-matrix analysis, but it selects between implementations — including the vendor library — rather than only among a single kernel's internal variants.

9. Limitations and Future Work

  • Large, highly irregular matrices. Our single hashing strategy is not tuned for very large / high-fill rows; a merge-based accumulator branch and finer intra-matrix (per-row-block) dispatch are the clear next steps.
  • H100 measurements, sm_80 code. Numbers should be reproduced natively on the A100; the relative gains are expected to hold or improve as cuSPARSE loses its Hopper-path advantage.
  • Heuristic vs learned dispatcher. The shallow tree is a stand-in for a principled, trained cost model over structural descriptors — a natural path to a stronger, more generalizable dispatcher.
  • Benchmark proxy. Group 1 proxies the official set by size regime; the exact official matrices may differ in content.

10. Conclusion

Real-world SpGEMM is fundamentally a generality problem. We built a correct (0/258 mismatches), portable, competitive hash engine for both challenge products, showed that neither it nor the vendor library wins everywhere, and turned that fact into a profiling-free, structure-aware dispatcher that reaches $2.63\times$ over cuSPARSE (88% of the oracle), driven by row-length imbalance. The approach is robust by construction: it routes each matrix to whatever solves it fastest — which is precisely what a general, real-world SpGEMM solver needs.
